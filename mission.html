The Ship of Theseus poses the question of whether a ship is still the same once every one of its planks has been replaced, one at a time.

Global trade is that ship. It stops for no one and answers to no one. It cannot be paused and relaunched; it can only be rebuilt while moving.

Hayek observed that the knowledge an economy runs on never exists in one place. It is scattered among the individuals who hold it. In trade, that knowledge lives in inboxes, phone calls and the memory of who knows who. Because knowledge is scattered, coordination is expensive. Because coordination is expensive, intermediaries appear. Brokers, forwarders and customs agents are not the purpose of trade: they are its planks.

Software will not remove the need for intermediation. Intermediaries exist to coordinate. When the cost of coordination falls, the planks can be replaced.

So, is the ship still the same? Aristotle would say a thing is defined by its form, not its matter. A ship is a ship because it carries. Trade is trade because it keeps promises: that goods will arrive, that they will be paid for, and that they are permitted to cross. The planks were never the ship.

Theseus keeps every promise and replaces every plank. The ship will still sail. It will simply sail faster.
